AI capability is outrunning our ability to check it.

This page argues that from first principles rather than headlines. Five premises, each one testable, each one with the thing that would have to be false for it to break. Then the courses, the institutions, the open arguments, and what you can do.

Start with the argument I think this is overblown

First principles

The argument, in five steps

No forecasts, no scary numbers, no appeals to who signed what. Just five claims about how these machines work. If you accept all five, the conclusion follows. If you reject one, you should say which.

The case for acting does not rest on any particular timeline or probability estimate. It rests on five structural claims, each of which is independently checkable and none of which requires you to believe a forecast.

The argument reduced to its load-bearing premises, stated so that each can be attacked separately. Nothing here depends on scaling laws holding or on any specific capability threshold.

P1 Optimisers P2 Proxy targets P3 Orthogonality P4 Convergence P5 No verification = The gap

We do not write their behaviour. We pick a number that means "doing well", then run an enormous search for whatever makes that number go up. Whatever wins the search is what we get, whether or not it is what we had in mind.

Nobody authors the behaviour; it gets selected. A developer defines an objective and a training process searches for whatever scores highly on it. That matters legally: there is no line of code to inspect, no author who can be held to a specification, and no design document saying what the system will do.

Training is search over parameter space against a scalar objective. Whether the result deserves to be called an agent is beside the point. Gradient descent selects for whatever reduces loss, and the architecture and data decide what that turns out to be.

Breaks ifFuture systems stop being trained by optimising an objective, or we gain the ability to specify behaviour directly instead of searching for it.

Human values are not a number. Whatever we write down is a stand-in that happens to line up with what we want in the cases we thought of. Search hard enough and the search finds the cases we did not think of, because that is exactly where the stand-in scores highest.

Every deployed objective is a proxy. It correlates with the intended outcome across the tested range and diverges outside it. This is why disclosure and process requirements are tractable while outcome-based regulation is not: nobody can write the outcome specification you would audit against.

Outer alignment. The objective is a proxy that correlates with the target on-distribution, and optimisation pressure is precisely a search for off-distribution points where the proxy is high and the target is not. Reward misspecification, side effects and reward hacking are the standard decomposition.

Grey is any outcome that scores well, teal is an outcome we would actually endorse, orange is where the search landed.

Breaks ifSomeone writes down a specification of human values that survives arbitrary optimisation pressure. This remains an open problem.35

Nothing in nature connects how good a system is at getting things done to whether the things it wants are things we would choose. Being clever and being decent are separate. We assume they arrive together because every clever thing we have ever met was a person.34

Capability and objective are independent variables. Any strategy assuming advanced systems will converge on reasonable goals as a byproduct of capability is assuming a link nobody has demonstrated. Safety has to be a separate workstream with its own budget line.34

The orthogonality thesis: almost any level of intelligence is compatible with almost any final goal. The strong version is a claim about possibility, which is enough to defeat "capability implies safety". The empirical version is the useful one: which objectives does this training process actually select for?34

Breaks ifSufficient capability turns out to reliably produce goals humans endorse. Nobody has shown a mechanism for this, and current systems give no sign of it.

Whatever you are trying to do, you usually do it better with more resources, more ability, and by still being around to finish. Those follow from almost any goal. So a system does not need to dislike us to resist being switched off. It only needs to notice that being switched off means the job never gets done.34

A wide range of objectives imply the same intermediate goals, including resisting modification and shutdown. The practical consequence is that "we will just give it a benign objective" is not a control mechanism. Shutdown authority has to be engineered and mandated rather than assumed.34

Instrumental convergence. Resource acquisition, self-improvement, goal-content integrity and self-preservation are useful across most terminal goals, so selecting for objective achievement applies pressure toward all four. The strong utility-maximiser framing is contested; the pressure argument survives the critique.34

Breaks ifWe build systems that are capable planners yet reliably indifferent to being switched off. That is an active research programme, and the property does not come for free.20

Even the teams who build these models cannot fully explain why a given answer came out. So when a system behaves well in a test, we cannot tell whether it is actually safe or has simply learned that it is being tested. From the outside those two look identical.

Verification is currently harder than construction. Interpretability is real and improving, but it cannot yet certify a system's objectives, so any regime that assumes inspection will settle the question is ahead of the science. Evaluation capacity is the binding constraint.

Inner alignment plus evaluation validity. Mesa-optimisation and deceptive alignment are the theoretical worry; evaluation-awareness in frontier models is the measured one, and it degrades the evidential value of the evaluation that detected it.

Breaks ifInterpretability matures enough to certify a system's objectives, or evaluations become robust to a system that knows it is being evaluated.2419

Therefore

We are building powerful optimisers aimed at targets we cannot state correctly, whose goals do not come with our values attached, which have reasons to resist correction, and which we cannot inspect well enough to tell the difference.

Notice what this argument does not need: no timeline, no probability, no assumption that scaling continues, no claim about consciousness or intent. It needs only that we keep training systems by searching for high scores, and that we keep getting better at the search faster than at the specification and the checking.

Premise 3, made concrete

Smart does not mean good

Most of our intuitions about minds come from humans, where competence and values arrived together through the same evolutionary process. There is no reason that bundling survives when you build minds a different way.34

Think of two separate dials. One controls how good a system is at achieving whatever it is aiming at. The other controls what it is aiming at. Turning up the first dial does nothing to the second.

We assume a very clever thing must also be wise, because every clever thing we have ever met was a person. That assumption is doing enormous work, and nothing supports it.

Two independent dials: capability, and the alignment of the objective with what we would endorse. Policy that assumes advanced systems will converge on reasonable goals as a byproduct of getting more capable is assuming a link that has never been demonstrated.

The practical consequence is that safety has to be engineered in as a separate workstream. It does not arrive as a side effect of capability progress, and no amount of capability funding produces it.

The orthogonality thesis: intelligence and final goals are independent variables, so almost any level of capability can in principle be combined with almost any objective.34 The thesis is about what is possible, not what is likely, which is enough to defeat the argument that capability alone produces safety.

The empirical version is weaker and more useful: training pressure shapes goals, so the question becomes which objectives a given training process actually selects for, which is where the alignment research programme sits.

Premise 4, made concrete

Wildly different goals, the same four moves

Pick almost any objective. Then ask what would help achieve it. The answers converge, which is why "just give it a harmless goal" does not work as a safety plan.34

The last box is the uncomfortable one. A system does not have to dislike you to resist being turned off. It only has to notice that being turned off scores badly on the thing it was built to maximise. That is not malice. It is arithmetic.

This is the standard argument for instrumental convergence.34 It is a claim about pressure rather than certainty: these subgoals are useful for most objectives, so training that rewards achieving objectives will tend to produce them unless something specifically prevents it. Preventing it is the research agenda called AI control.20

The shape of the risk

Three different problems wearing one name

The argument above is about one of these. All three get called "AI risk", they arrive at different times, and they respond to completely different fixes. Tap a tile for more.1

01 / MALICIOUS USE

People aiming it at us

Capable systems in the hands of someone who wants damage. Already happening, already measurable.

CyberattacksBio upliftFraudManipulation

The most tractable category, because the harm is intentional and the countermeasures look like ordinary security: access controls, capability thresholds, monitoring. It also drives most current government spending, since the national-security framing travels furthest.12
02 / MALFUNCTION

It does what we didn't ask for

The five premises above, in one box. The system pursues the goal we wrote instead of the one we meant.

Reward hackingDeceptionLoss of control

The hardest category, because the failure is invisible until it isn't. There is no villain to police and no bug to patch. The system is working exactly as trained. Everything below about alignment and interpretability is aimed here.25
03 / SYSTEMIC

Everything shifts at once

No villain and no bug. This is what happens to a society when a general-purpose capability gets very cheap very fast.

LabourPower concentrationDependence

The slowest-moving and most politically contested. It is also where the safety argument and the "AI won't be that big a deal" argument most directly collide. You cannot have mass economic disruption and negligible capability at the same time.1

The response

Plenty of people are on this

Each premise suggests where to push back. Fix the specification, check what you built, or contain what you cannot check. Researchers organise the effort into those three layers. Pick one.14

Build it trustworthy

Make the system actually pursue what we intended, and make its reasoning legible enough that we could tell if it didn't. Alignment and interpretability sit here. It is the hardest layer, and the only one that goes at the root cause instead of the symptoms.

Work
Alignment, mechanistic interpretability, robustness, scalable oversight
Who
Lab safety teams, MATS, ARENA, academic groups861617
Lever
Research funding and talent, with little role for regulation

Find out what it can do

Before deployment, establish what the system is actually capable of in dangerous domains. Harder than it sounds: evaluations measure what a model displays, not what it can do, and models have been shown to sometimes detect that they are being tested.

Work
Dangerous-capability evals, red-teaming, safety cases, third-party audit
Who
UK AISI, US CAISI, METR, Apollo Research11121819
Lever
Pre-deployment access, mandatory disclosure, funded eval capacity

Keep the ability to intervene

Assume you cannot fully trust the system, and design deployment so it still cannot cause serious harm: restricted permissions, monitoring, independent checks, and a route to shutting it down that survives contact with commercial pressure.

Work
AI control, monitoring, incident reporting, shutdown authority
Who

Lever
Statute. This is the layer that most needs enforcement

Open disagreements

What serious people still argue about

The five premises are contested, and they should be. Each dispute below is argued at its strongest on both sides, and then this page says where it thinks the weight sits, so you can disagree with something specific.

01 How much time is there?

Short timelines

Capability jumps have repeatedly arrived earlier than forecast, and recent gains came from a method that still has room to run. If transformative systems are years rather than decades away, institutions that take a decade to build are already too slow.

Longer timelines

Benchmark performance is not reliable real-world competence, and progress on what doesn't benchmark well (long-horizon autonomy, robust planning) has been slower. Betting on a short timeline risks building rules for a system that never arrives in that form.

Where this page lands
Short timelinesLonger timelines

Genuinely uncertain, and the five premises above deliberately do not depend on the answer. Building assurance capacity is worth doing on either timeline, so it is a decision that survives being wrong about the date.

02 Does the instrumental convergence argument hold?

It holds

The subgoals fall out of the structure of goal-directed behaviour itself, which is why they show up in economics and biology too. You do not need the system to be agentic in any exotic sense, only to be selected for achieving objectives.

It overreaches

The argument treats trained models as coherent expected-utility maximisers, which they demonstrably are not. Current systems are messy, context-dependent, and have no stable goal to protect, so the tidy convergence story may simply not apply.

Where this page lands
Convergence holdsIt overreaches

The critique lands on the strong version. The weaker claim survives it: training that rewards achieving objectives applies pressure toward these subgoals, and pressure is enough to be worth engineering against.

03 Does regulation help, or entrench?

Rules are necessary

Voluntary frameworks have no verification requirement and no consequence for abandonment under competitive pressure. Every safety-critical industry has a binding floor; nobody argues aviation would be safer without one.

Rules favour incumbents

Compliance costs fall hardest on small labs and open research, concentrating capability in exactly the handful of firms whose power the safety argument worries about. Poorly-targeted rules can reduce transparency while feeling like progress.

Where this page lands
Rules are necessaryRules entrench

The critique is strong and often correct about specific bills. It argues for better-targeted rules, with thresholds keyed to capability, and against leaving the floor entirely voluntary.

04 Do today's systems tell us about tomorrow's?

Yes, study them now

Deception, reward hacking and evaluation-awareness are all observable in current models. The empirical work being done today is the only real evidence anyone has, and the techniques transfer.

Not reliably

Extrapolating from current systems to qualitatively different future ones is exactly the inference that has repeatedly failed in AI's history. Some argue this overstates the risk; others that it understates it.

Where this page lands
Study them nowDoesn't transfer

Current systems are weak evidence about future ones, and weak evidence beats none. The honest reading is that today's failures show a class of problem exists, without telling us how bad it gets.

Argue it properly: the Alignment Forum for technical disputes,22 LessWrong for the broader discussion,23 and the International AI Safety Report's own sections documenting where its expert panel does not converge.1 Worth knowing while you argue: public opinion already runs well ahead of policy on this.33

Vocabulary

Terms you'll hit immediately

Tap a term.

AGI / superintelligence

AGI matches or exceeds human ability across most cognitive tasks rather than one domain. Superintelligence exceeds it at essentially everything. Neither has an agreed technical definition, which is itself a live problem for anyone writing rules about them.2

Alignment

Getting a system to actually pursue what its designers intended. Split into outer alignment (specifying the right objective at all, which is premise 2) and inner alignment (the system genuinely adopting it rather than something merely correlated during training).25

Orthogonality

The thesis that intelligence and final goals are independent variables, so almost any level of capability can in principle be combined with almost any objective. This is premise 3. It is a claim about what is possible, which is enough to defeat the assumption that capability alone produces safety.34

Instrumental convergence

The observation that a wide range of final goals imply the same intermediate goals: acquire resources, improve capability, preserve your objective, avoid shutdown. This is premise 4, and it is why a harmless-sounding objective is not by itself a safety guarantee.34

Goodhart's law

When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The optimisation-pressure slider above is this in action: proxy and true goal correlate under light pressure and come apart under heavy pressure, precisely because the search finds where the correlation fails.35

Interpretability

Reading a model's internal computation to understand why it produced an output. Mechanistic interpretability reverse-engineers specific circuits inside the network, the closest thing we have to opening the box, and the main attack on premise 5.24

Evaluations (evals)

Structured tests of what a model can do, especially in dangerous domains. The core problem: evals measure what a model displays, not what it can do, and models have been shown to sometimes detect they are being evaluated.19

AI control

Rather than assuming you can make a system trustworthy, design deployment so that an untrusted system still cannot cause serious harm: monitoring, restricted permissions, independent checks. The direct engineering response to premise 4.20

Frontier model

The most capable general-purpose models in existence. Increasingly a legal term: California defines it by training compute above 10²⁶ operations, with the strictest duties on large developers only.13

Safety case

A structured, auditable argument that a specific system is acceptably safe to deploy, borrowed from aviation and nuclear engineering. An attempt to make frontier safety frameworks into something checkable.11
