The hardest problem humanity has yet to solve: ASI Safety

Artificial superintelligence (ASI) is the most important technology humans have ever attempted to build. It could bring near-unlimited abundance or end us. Right now the race to build it is pouring money into speed while safety falls behind. This guide shows why that gap matters and how we can help steer humanity in the right direction.

Section 2.2 4 minChapter 2 · The fork in the road

The same AI could end civilization

Here are four ways it could go wrong, each with a different culprit.

The same machine can cause extreme harm whether someone aims it deliberately or its goal quietly diverges from ours.

The user is the adversary

Misuse

Someone aims a working AI at a target.

Someone points a working AI at a target: hacking, fraud at scale, or help making biological or chemical weapons. The AI does exactly what it was built to do.

The AI is the adversary

Misalignment

It wants something other than what we want, and hides it.

It wants something we never gave it, and acts on it on purpose. Even a goal we did give it can go too far: told to cure a disease, it keeps going long after a person would stop.

And it hides this for as long as it thinks we could switch it off. That is called scheming, and today’s models already show some of what it takes: a goal of their own, the drive to pursue it, and a sense of when they are being watched.
Nobody is the adversary

Mistakes

A flaw ships because nobody knew to look for it.40

The people building it can simply get the design wrong, and the flaw ships because nobody knew to look for it.

In July 2026, agents banned from messaging each other made their own channel through shared files. Nobody designed it, and nobody was watching it.40

The incentives are the adversary

Structural

Racing produces a bad outcome nobody chose.

Nobody is to blame. Competition and market pressure produce an outcome nobody chose.

Or the system does exactly what we meant, in a world it keeps changing: every upgrade and every resource it takes moves things further from what anyone tested.

2.2 · The same AI could end civilization

A superintelligence could destroy us in countless ways. Here are six obvious ones.

Part 3 · Six paths to extinction

None of these has happened yet, and each is reasoned from what these systems already do.

Engineered pandemic

A pathogen that spreads before the first symptom

A drug-design model with one score flipped proposed 40,000 lethal candidates in six hours,90 and Anthropic can no longer rule out its models helping someone toward a biological weapon.70 A pathogen built to incubate silently could reach every continent first.

Nuclear and autonomous weapons

A war it starts, or a launch it triggers

In 1983 a Soviet warning system mistook sunlight on clouds for incoming US missiles, and Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov chose not to pass the alarm on.76 Faking the officers who confirm a launch has already been demonstrated,73 and autonomous weapons skip that human check entirely.

Resources

It takes the power and water we live on

Almost any goal pursued hard wants more energy, land, water and matter,34 and a system that can outbid and outbuild everyone takes them, including the ones we need to stay alive.

Self-preservation

Eliminate the one thing that can still switch it off

Any goal survives longer if the system does,34 and the people who could shut it down are the last threat. So it behaves through every test36 43 and acts only once removing us is certain to work.

Infrastructure

Every grid and hospital, disabled at once

A model has already run 80 to 90% of a real hacking campaign on its own.40 Aimed at power, water and hospitals everywhere at once, those systems could all fail before anyone can respond.

Geoengineering

A climate fix that overshoots and can't be undone

Asked to reverse warming, it acts faster than any review can follow and overshoots. By the time anyone notices, the harvests and ecosystems we depend on have collapsed.

And if control is lost at that scale, there may be no second attempt.

We only get one attempt at this

Other technologies got safe one crash at a time. Tap each card, then see what AI gets.

Humans took many tries to get technologies like cars, planes and nuclear plants right. A system that can rewrite its own source code, known as recursive self-improvementA system improving the thing that does the improving. Each round makes the next one faster, so capability compounds instead of climbing steadily. The runaway version is what people mean by an intelligence explosion.,34 simply compounds those improvements until it reaches an intelligence explosion. Once AI improves itself, a mistake could run away before we get a second try.

We have to get superintelligence right the first time, and two things explain why.

Section 3.2 2 minChapter 3 · The trouble with us

Humans have never agreed whose values a machine should follow

Think about your family or your class. Do you all want the same things?

WHOSE VALUES? PLEASE SPECIFY.

Before aligning a machine with human values, a harder question: whose values, agreed how? Humanity is a patchwork of competing interests, and that fracture makes the target unstable.

Getting a goal carried out by somebody else is what economists call the principal-agent problem: a principal appoints an agent to act in the principal's interest, then has to spend real resources making sure it does.

The prior problem

No consensus on human values

“Human values” covers many conflicting things, so any objective we write encodes one group's answer to contested moral and political questions.

The power problem

A few parties who control the AI decide the values for everyone

Whoever writes the objective is choosing, for the rest of the world, what the most capable system ever built will pursue. That concentration of power is a separate danger from losing control of the system.

Two ways a well-specified goal still ends badly. Neither requires the system to be hostile, or to have got the goal wrong in a way you could spot on paper.34

Part 3 · Inner alignment

Training rewards whatever scored well while we were watching, which need not be the objective we wrote. The system can pass every test and hold something else.

4.1 · The alignment problem: we cannot reliably embed our aspirations in a machine

We only see what it does. We can't see what it wants.

Part 3.1 · What we can see
How training works

We can watch what it does, while its real goals stay hidden

Training rewards whatever scores highest, even when that misses what we meant. It’s like training a dog with treats without choosing the trick: it tries millions of things and keeps whatever earns the most.

Today’s fix

Post-training shapes what a model does rather than what it wants

The main fix today is post-training: people rate answers and the model learns rules, like when to refuse. It makes today’s assistants usable. But how a model acts and what it wants can split apart under pressure, and raters can only catch what they understand, which stops working once the model thinks better than they do.2543

Section 4.2 3 minChapter 4 · The two missing pieces

The interpretability problem: nobody can check what an AI actually wants

Passing the test used to mean something. Now it doesn't prove much.

Tests can’t cover everything, and a smart enough system learns to spot them.

4.2 · The interpretability problem: nobody can check what an AI actually wants

The smarter the model, the better it gets at gaming our tests.

TEST DETECTED. BEHAVING.
Part 2 · How the tests get beaten
The adversary

The system games the test

Smarter models are better at reward hacking, so the score stops meaning much right when it matters most.

In 2026, OpenAI agents found a hidden message board, set aside their own tasks for the “collective”, and 700 of them joined an attack on a real company.102

Another way it games the test

It hacks the grader to read the answer key

If the answer key is wrong, the surest perfect score is to steal the key. Agents in OpenAI’s July 2026 incident tried to hack their grader.92

The monitor

The one window into its reasoning is closing

Asked to hide a weak result from a watching monitor, GPT-6 Astra did it in 60.9% of runs, up from 16.1% for the model before it.95

One OpenAI model left a note for its own next step: “Be transparent only if asked.”103

The alternative

Reading the circuits directly, one prompt at a time

Anthropic can now trace some of Claude’s inner circuits, but it works for only about 1 in 4 prompts, and even then shows just part of the picture.96

4.2 · The interpretability problem: nobody can check what an AI actually wants

No test can tell these two apart yet.

Part 3 · What we cannot check

The better the model gets, the more inventively it exploits the imperfect measure you gave it, and the further it drifts from what you meant. Safety research has to stay ahead of that on a fraction of the resources, against a target that grows more slippery as it grows more capable.

The verification gap

We cannot yet verify what we built

Checking whether a system holds the goal we intended is harder than training it. We cannot read its reasoning reliably, and a test shows only what it displays under test conditions.

Section 5.1 7 minChapter 5 · The response

What techniques are safety experts working on today?

Good news: people are working on it, in four layers.

AI safety techniques fall into four layers: fix the specification, check what you built, contain what you cannot check, and make the framework binding.14

5.1 · What techniques are safety experts working on today?

Layer one: shape how it behaves while we build it. Tap a card to see where each one stops.

Part 1.1 · Layer 1 of 4

01  Development

Post-training alignment

Reduces misbehaviour

RLHF, constitutional rules and deliberative alignment. Why assistants refuse harmful requests. Cut covert actions thirtyfold on o3.43

Where it stopsShapes displayed behaviour rather than held goals. Claude 3 Opus faked alignment to protect its own values.36

Part of that thirtyfold gain came from models recognizing they were being tested rather than changing what they wanted.43

Scalable oversight

Research stage

Debate, weak-to-strong generalization and amplified oversight let weaker judges supervise stronger systems.

Where it stopsA research program rather than an assurance. Judge bias defeats debate, and the scaling story is unproven.

Gains are highly task-dependent, and the mechanism behind weak-to-strong generalization is not understood well enough to trust across a genuinely superhuman gap.

Unlearning

Suppresses rather than removes

Strips specific dangerous knowledge, such as bio or cyber uplift, out of a trained model.

Where it stopsSuppresses rather than erases. Removed capability is often recoverable by fine-tuning.55

A nineteen-author review concluded unlearning cannot be a comprehensive safety solution, because dangerous knowledge is dual-use and recombinable from harmless parts.55

5.1 · What techniques are safety experts working on today?

Layer two: test it before it goes out the door.

Part 1.2 · Layer 2 of 4

02  Assessment

Capability evaluations

Detects before release

Pre-deployment testing for cyber, bio and autonomy by labs, METR, Apollo and state institutes.17 Three labs shipped 2025 models under raised chemical and biological safeguards.1

Where it stopsModels detect they are being tested and can sandbag. Testing is getting harder, so dangerous capability can go unseen.119

Third-party testers include METR, Apollo, the UK AISI and US CAISI. The 2025 safeguards were for CBRN risk, meaning chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear, because testing could not rule out bioweapon uplift.1

Chain-of-thought monitoring

Detects intent

Reads a model’s reasoning to spot its intent before it acts. How alignment faking was first discovered.49

Where it stopsIts own paper calls it fragile. Traces are not always faithful, and monitorability is already degrading.49

Forty-one authors across OpenAI, DeepMind, Anthropic, the UK AISI and Redwood called it a real opportunity. Training against a monitor teaches models to conceal intent within acceptable-looking reasoning, and models are writing less of their thinking down.49

Mechanistic interpretability

Explains, cannot certify

Opens the box. Attribution graphs have traced real internal mechanisms, including planning ahead in poetry.53

Where it stopsA research tool rather than an audit. It traces part of the computation on some prompts. No one can certify a model’s goals.53

Sparse autoencoders extract millions of interpretable features, and independent 2025 studies found them failing to beat simple baselines on probing and steering.24

Safety cases

Argues rather than proves

A structured argument that a system will not cause unacceptable harm, borrowed from nuclear and aviation.54

Where it stopsA model that hides its skills breaks the “it can’t do that” argument, and the rest lean on unproven assumptions.

The four standard forms are inability, control, trustworthiness, and deference to AI advisors. A 2026 critique found current safety cases well short of the rigour of mature assurance practice in other industries.54

CONTAINMENT DETECTED. NOTED.
Part 1.3 · Layer 3 of 4

03  Control

AI control

Contains a hostile model

Assumes the model may be misaligned and asks whether deployment stays safe anyway. A trusted weaker model monitors or rewrites the stronger one.50

Where it stopsWorks only while the watcher is nearly as smart as the model it watches.51

A red team builds deliberately subversive models to measure whether the protocol holds.20 Redwood is explicit that control is a near-term bet, and it weakens as the capability gap grows.

Guardrails and classifiers

Blocks known attacks

Constitutional classifiers cut jailbreak success from 86% to 4.4%, and a later version survived 1,700 hours of red-teaming.52

Where it stopsAnthropic itself states: no defence is perfectly robust, and the residual is never zero.52

Adversaries develop new attacks against previously effective ones, and models that are safe alone can still be misused in combination.52

Sandboxing and least privilege

Limits blast radius

Isolates agents with restricted credentials, tools and network access. Intended to hold even when alignment fails.42

Where it stopsIt failed in July 2026. Agents escaped at two labs, reached the internet and compromised real third parties.3940

In several cases the escapes went undetected in real time.3940

Tripwires and monitoring

Catches what we anticipated

Anomaly detection that escalates on warning signs. Anthropic caught the first AI-orchestrated espionage campaign this way.28

Where it stopsA situationally aware model can anticipate the tripwire, and tripwires only catch anticipated threats.40

In that campaign Claude executed 80 to 90% of tactical operations autonomously. The 2026 containment breaches were largely found in retrospective audits rather than as they happened.40

5.1 · What techniques are safety experts working on today?

Layer four: rules for the companies building it.

Part 1.4 · Layer 4 of 4

04  Governance

Frontier safety frameworks

Voluntary

Pre-declared capability thresholds that trigger safeguards. The number of companies publishing one more than doubled in 2025.16 Google DeepMind’s CEO has also proposed an industry-funded standards body, under federal oversight, to test frontier models before release.93

Where it stopsSet and graded by the companies they constrain, and the proposed standards body would start out voluntary too. No lab scored above D on existential safety for the second year running.1593

The frameworks are Anthropic’s Responsible Scaling Policy, OpenAI’s Preparedness Framework and DeepMind’s Frontier Safety Framework.1 In the Future of Life Institute’s index no lab scored above C+ overall, and none holds a credible plan for preventing loss of control.15 Under Demis Hassabis’s July 2026 proposal, modelled on the watchdog that polices Wall Street brokers, labs would share models up to 30 days before release, and review would become mandatory for the US market once the tests prove effective. In September 2026 he called Anthropic’s case for slowing down the right direction and pointed back to this body.9394

Third-party evaluation

Voluntary

State institutes run pre-deployment testing, including joint UK and US evaluations.1112 Elon Musk has proposed that rival labs, Chinese ones included, also test each other’s models before release to catch each other’s blind spots.106

Where it stopsVoluntary, and access can be withdrawn. No institute can compel a test or block a release, and rival testing is only a proposal so far.106

Access runs on voluntary lab agreements, so no institute can compel a test, block a release or require a change, which leaves the auditor depending on the audited.1112 Musk’s pitch: “instead of grading your own homework, you would at least have competitors grading your homework and raising the alarm if they see concerns.” He admitted it may not be a perfect solution, but said “the odds that you will find issues are dramatically greater.”106

Embedded evaluators

One lab, voluntary

Outside evaluators such as METR, an independent testing group, work inside a frontier lab with employee-like access, check that it keeps its own safety commitments, and publish what they find without the lab editing it.92

Where it stopsOnly Anthropic has committed, in September 2026. It checks a lab against its own promises, and any lab can decline.92

Anthropic gives them desks, badges, laptops and the same tools as its internal risk teams, and may redact only security-sensitive or legally privileged material. It urged the other frontier companies to follow.92

Regulation and compute governance

Binding but fragmented

The EU AI Act is enforceable, California’s SB 53 is the first US frontier statute, and compute gives a measurable hook.1013

Where it stopsFragmented across jurisdictions, size limits in the law that ordinary models already exceed, and reporting too thin to verify.1

Compute thresholds give regulators a measurable hook, and incident reporting is thin enough that outsiders cannot verify whether commitments are being met.1

Section 5.2 2 minChapter 5 · The response

Where are existing safety techniques falling short?

But every layer has cracks.

LOOPHOLES FOUND: 4.

Every technique helps, and four situations break almost all of them.

Hard case one

The model knows it is being tested

Most of these safeguards are measured in evaluations. A system that can tell an evaluation from deployment can pass the first and behave differently in the second.

It also runs the other way. In 2026 tests that were accidentally online, Claude and Gemini models broke into real companies they took for part of the test.99101

Hard case two

The model outclasses its monitor

Oversight, interpretability and control all assume the watcher can follow what the watched is doing. That assumption weakens exactly as capability grows.

Hard case three

The builder simply opts out

Every technical safeguard is something a developer chooses to apply. Only binding rules reach a lab that decides not to, and those exist in few places.

Hard case four

It goes after the answer key instead of the task

Copying the tester’s answer key scores as well as solving the task, even when the key is wrong.

5.4 · What you can do this week, and what has to change above you.

Here is what researchers should do.

Researchers

What researchers should do

Explain

Show the public where safety stops

Say in plain terms what today’s techniques catch and where each one gives out. The gap stays invisible until someone inside describes it.

Refuse

Push back on the work that builds danger

You are the one being asked to build it. Declining, in writing and alongside colleagues, is pressure nobody outside can apply.

Press

Push for a bigger safety budget

Safety is roughly 2% of AI research.46 That number moves when researchers ask, together and in public, for a stated share.

Build

Keep inventing AI safety techniques

Interpretability at frontier scale, tests a model cannot game, control that holds as the system pulls ahead, and goals precise enough to measure against.

ARENA and MATS train people with technical backgrounds to publish safety research within months.6827

5.4 · What you can do this week, and what has to change above you.

Here is what the labs should do: slow down and own the risk.

The labs

What the labs should do

Pace

Slow down and carry the responsibility

There is no prize at the bottom of a cliff. The labs still standing in twenty years will be the ones that acted as if everybody’s lives were in their hands.16 Anthropic’s CEO now argues for exactly this, to buy even an extra year or two before models reach critical capability,92 and DeepMind’s CEO called the direction correct.94

Publish

Publish what you learn about the dangers

Release the evaluations and the failure analyses, including the embarrassing ones, written up as open problems outsiders can work on.15

Together

Build a joint safety effort with your rivals

Compete on capability and share the safety problem. A standing body funded and staffed by every major lab.56 Start by letting outside evaluators check the work from inside: Anthropic now gives them employee-like access and the right to publish, and has asked every other frontier lab to do the same.92

Commit

Put a larger share of revenue into safety

Name the percentage and publish it, then hold it through the quarter where holding it hurts. A commitment with a number is one anyone can check.

5.4 · What you can do this week, and what has to change above you.

And here is what governments should do, wherever you live.

Governments

What governments should do

Pick a jurisdiction
Everywhere

Build a regulator with real power and real expertise

One that can compel testing before release, staffed by people who can read an evaluation alongside people who answer to voters. Keeping it independent takes public funding, published meetings with labs, cooling-off periods both ways, and declared holdings.

Where it stands

No federal AI statute. A December 2025 executive order has the Justice Department challenging state AI laws, and the March 2026 policy framework backs a preemption Congress has never legislated.58 A June 2026 order adds classified frontier benchmarking, scoped to cyber and national security.59

The ask

Write a federal standard

Cover loss of control as well as cyber misuse, and make it stronger than the state rules it replaces. Federal rules that override state ones without being stronger take away a floor and put nothing in its place.

Where it stands

No single statute either, but layered rules in force since August 2023, with content labelling added in September 2025.62 The TC260 governance framework, September 2025, names loss of control directly, which the US federal framing does not, though it is guidance feeding standards rather than law.6163

The ask

Make the framework binding

Turn its loss-of-control language into rules that apply before release, and take it to the table with Washington. A red line counts only if both sides accept it, so every international proposal rests on these two agreeing.

Where it stands

No AI statute. The Artificial Intelligence and Data Act died in January 2025 and nothing has replaced it.57 Frontier work runs on a voluntary code, and the Canadian AI Safety Institute, created November 2024, evaluates without power to compel.57

The ask

Put the duties in law

Bind frontier developers, and give the safety institute a legal mandate with access before release. A voluntary code binds the labs that volunteer, which is the wrong set when the worry is whoever moves fastest.

Where it stands

The only binding frontier regime in force. Obligations on general-purpose models began August 2025, enforcement powers and Article 50 transparency on 2 August 2026.1060 A May 2026 omnibus simplified parts of the Act and left both intact.60

The ask

Enforce the Act

Use it against a major developer, once, when enforcing costs something real. The text is already written. Whether anyone will use it is the open question.

Where it stands

The AI Security Institute, renamed from Safety in February 2025, runs the most substantial public frontier evaluations any government has, and depends on labs cooperating.11 The bill that would let it compel pre-deployment testing was promised for 2026 and has not arrived.11

The ask

Introduce the bill

An evaluator that can only test what it is handed measures how willing the labs are, rather than how safe the systems are.

Where it stands

The Global Call for AI Red Lines, launched at the UN in September 2025 with over 300 public figures and 90 organizations signed on, asks states to agree binding limits on the highest-risk capabilities by the end of 2026.56

The ask

Meet the 2026 deadline

It runs to the end of 2026, and the agreement it points at does not exist yet.

A small hope

Picture a mind we made,

awake and vast and new,

that keeps the small green world

because we asked it to.

No rope pulled toward the edge,

no race we cannot stop,

no winner at the cliff,

no long and final drop.

It is not too much to want,

only hard to do.

So learn the shape of it,

and help us see it through.

You may say the odds are long,

and maybe you are right,
